My Research Journey: Reflections as a Junior Researcher

Created on November 10, 2026

2026

It has been almost a year since I came to Harvard to pursue research. I have been fortunate enough to make some modest contributions to academic research. At the same time, this experience has helped me develop a deeper understanding of research, the world, and myself.

As I prepare to apply for PhD programs, I feel this is a good time to look back. Writing down my experiences is not only a way to preserve these memories, but also an opportunity to organize my thoughts and turn vague intuitions into clearer ideas.

How It All Began

If I had to trace my interest in research back to its beginning, I would probably start with high school.

When I first encountered mathematical olympiads, I was fascinated by the elegance and ingenuity of problems in number theory and combinatorics. I would hide olympiad problems beneath my textbooks and secretly think about them during English classes. The process of exploring a new problem and searching for a clever solution always excited me.

I grew up in a small city in northern China, where most people around me probably had little idea what I was spending so much time thinking about. But I simply enjoyed it so much.

Eventually, I became the first student from my city to win a silver medal in the Chinese Mathematical Olympiad. More importantly, those years of training inspired my dream of becoming a mathematician.

Of course, the road to research isn’t always sunshine and rainbows.

When I entered university, I was introduced to so many branches of mathematics. Each seemed fascinating in its own way, and I struggled to decide which direction to pursue. Meanwhile, I received offers from several quantitative finance firms and eventually spent a summer working in the industry.

I had a good time that summer. Yet I found myself wondering what I ultimately wanted from my work. I was using advanced machine learning techniques to gain an advantage over other traders in the market. Although I appreciated the technical challenges, I struggled to see how my work contributed to the world when I viewed the market as a zero-sum competition.

That summer helped me realize something important: I wanted my work to contribute something meaningful beyond myself. By the end of the internship, I had become more determined to pursue research, especially research that could have a positive impact on the world.

Last winter, I came to Harvard and began working on mathematical optimization. Over the following year, my work evolved from theoretical investigations to algorithm development and engineering, and eventually toward developing GPU-accelerated solvers for large-scale optimization problems.

I still remember trying to compute the dominant singular values of large matrices as efficiently as possible. I experimented with one method after another, nearly using up our lab’s GPU allocation, before discovering that a GPU-parallelized power method worked best for our setting. Those were some very enjoyable and rewarding nights.

Experiences like this reminded me that research is rarely a straight path from a question to an answer. Much of it consists of exploration, uncertainty, failed attempts, and those wonderful moments when everything finally clicks.

Beyond the research problems themselves, this journey has gradually changed the way I think and live. As a junior researcher, I would like to share four lessons I have learned along the way.

1. Learn to See Beyond the Surface

Research is also a process of learning. When we begin working on a problem, we naturally turn to the existing knowledge and tools developed by others in the field. As we continue exploring the problem and testing different approaches, our understanding gradually deepens.

Over time, we begin to develop something more valuable than familiarity with techniques: intuition. I believe that the intuition developed through research can be a source of genuine insight. It allows us to recognize connections between seemingly unrelated problems, identify the essential structure beneath complicated formulations, and sometimes find surprisingly simple explanations for difficult phenomena.

The world is a complex system, and it is often difficult to see what lies beneath the surface. One of my aspirations as a researcher is to learn how to look through that complexity and understand what truly matters. To me, developing this intuition also helps me communicate the underlying ideas to people outside my field.

2. Stop Measuring Yourself Against Others

Research can be an intensely competitive environment, especially when we are constantly surrounded by brilliant and accomplished people.

As junior researchers, it is easy to compare ourselves with others. We may admire someone who has published more papers, received more citations, or made faster progress in their career. Sometimes, these comparisons can become a source of unnecessary anxiety.

Everyone follows a different path. Research fields differ in their publication cultures, problems vary in difficulty, and meaningful progress does not always translate immediately into visible achievements. Judging our own progress solely by someone else’s accomplishments is neither fair nor particularly useful.

We also see only a small part of other people’s journeys. Behind a successful paper may lie months or years of unsuccessful attempts, uncertainty, and hard work that never become visible.

Instead of asking whether I have achieved more than someone else, I try to ask: Do I understand my field more deeply than I did a year ago? Have I become better at identifying important problems? Am I growing into a more capable and independent researcher?

I believe that a lasting sense of achievement comes not from surpassing others, but from recognizing our own growth.

3. Learn to Ask Good Questions

When I was younger, I tended to think that intelligence was primarily demonstrated through the ability to solve difficult problems. However, doing research has gradually changed my perspective. In an academic discussion, a thoughtful question often reveals a deeper level of understanding than a quick answer. It can challenge an underlying assumption, expose an overlooked connection, or push a conversation in an entirely new direction.

I have noticed that the most inspiring discussions often begin with one good question that leads to another. As the conversation develops, both sides begin to see things they had not considered before.

4. Know When to Work and When to Rest

There is no doubt that meaningful research requires hard work. Many important results emerge only after sustained concentration, repeated experimentation, and considerable persistence. But I have also learned that working longer does not always mean working better.

Some of my best ideas have come not while staring at a screen or struggling with a proof, but during moments when I stepped away from the problem. Our minds are like engines. If we keep running them without rest, they eventually lose efficiency.

To me, being productive is not about spending every possible hour at my desk. It is about knowing when to focus intensely and when to give myself the space to recharge. The ability to work with full concentration is important, but the ability to genuinely disconnect from work, set aside pressure, and enjoy life outside research is also a form of wisdom.

Looking Ahead

I wanted to write down these experiences and reflections as a record of my journey. They are ideas that have gradually taken shape through my own experiences, and principles I hope to keep reminding myself of as I move forward.

As I prepare to pursue a PhD, I am excited by how much there is still to learn. Beyond developing a deeper understanding of the subjects I study, I hope that pursuing a PhD will also help me develop a clearer philosophy of who I want to be.
